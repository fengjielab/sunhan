\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Principal Findings and Interpretation of the Five-Mode Design}
The five-mode evaluation produced one primary finding and several supporting descriptive observations. Mode C had the shortest task completion time among the five tested modes. In the predefined primary C--E comparison, complete multi-channel configuration was associated with a 1.79-s (8.5\%) lower mean task completion time, with a matched-block bootstrap 95\% confidence interval of 1.10--2.51 s. The mean C--E difference favored mode C for all three operators and all six tested objects. Observed success rate and Raw NASA-TLX also showed descriptive patterns favoring mode C, while the C--E trajectory-length confidence interval included zero. Together, these findings support a within-sample task-level advantage of complete multi-channel configuration under the tested conditions.

The five-mode design helps locate the level at which this advantage should be interpreted. The C--A contrast benchmarks the complete configuration against fixed-parameter operation. The C--B contrast compares automatic strategy selection with a timed manual-selection workflow that includes object identification and button-based strategy selection. Modes C--E used the same visual-semantic display throughout each trial. Mode D displayed the same object class, mapped strategy, confidence score, and bounding box as mode C while retaining the initialized parameter state. The C--D result therefore supports an additional task-level contribution from parameter configuration beyond semantic display alone.

The primary C--E contrast provides the most specific system-level comparison. Both modes used the same object-class-to-strategy mapping, visual-semantic display, and slave-side impedance update, while mode C additionally configured the master-side haptic and gripper-execution channels. The C--E result therefore supports an additional system-level contribution from coordinating the master-side haptic and gripper-execution channels with slave-side impedance. Because the two added channels changed together, this comparison is interpreted at the bundled system level.

The C--E trajectory-length estimate was small, and its bootstrap confidence interval included zero. Mode C showed a lower mean pause count, although the two modes had the same median pause count. No confirmatory analysis was performed for the pause measure. Taken together, the process measures suggest that the time reduction may be associated with fewer corrective interruptions in some trials, while the aggregate geometric path length changed little. This process-level interpretation remains exploratory.

\subsection{Asynchronous Perception--Control Integration}
Because semantic output was used for parameter reconfiguration only when generating the one-shot strategy event, the perception process did not need to operate at the nominal 200-Hz supervisory rate. In the implemented multi-rate architecture, 15-fps image acquisition and independent visual inference communicated with the nominal 200-Hz supervisory loop through bounded queues. The single-slot frame queue limited stale-frame accumulation, while non-blocking polling allowed the supervisory loop to retrieve available inference results without waiting for the inference process to complete.

The mean per-image processing time of 48.19 ms was substantially longer than one nominal supervisory period, whereas the median logged supervisory-cycle duration was 5.07 ms. This difference in timescales highlights the role of asynchronous decoupling in the present architecture. Accordingly, the implemented architecture was designed for one-shot strategy configuration followed by strategy locking; continuous visual servoing was outside the implemented control structure.

The implementation supports the feasibility of asynchronous, non-blocking integration of low-rate semantic perception into the supervisory software architecture. The reported timing results characterize software-level integration and typical module timing. Hard real-time guarantees and end-to-end trigger-to-contact timing remain uncharacterized.

\subsection{Variation Across Operators and Objects}
The positive mean C--E completion-time difference was observed for all three operators and persisted in the leave-one-operator-out analyses. Mode C also had a lower mean task completion time than mode E for each of the six tested objects, with descriptive reductions ranging from 3.3\% to 13.2\%. Together, these directional checks show that the aggregate C--E difference was distributed across operators and objects in the observed dataset. The operator- and object-level summaries are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:8}.

The magnitude of the C--E difference nevertheless varied across objects, with smaller reductions for the bottle and banana and larger reductions for the paper cup and scissors. This pattern may be related to differences in grasp-pose requirements, deformation sensitivity, and the need to retain object orientation during transfer. The larger reductions for the paper cup and scissors are compatible with greater value from complete multi-channel configuration when deformation management or orientation retention becomes more demanding. Each object-level summary was based on four or five matched task blocks and was therefore treated descriptively. Targeted experiments with larger per-object samples and additional objects are needed to determine whether the observed variation follows specific object or task properties.

\subsection{Relation to Previous Work and Mechatronic Design Implications}
Prior tele-impedance and variable-impedance studies have adapted slave-side impedance using human-state information, contact-force information, demonstrations, or task phase \cite{ref06,ref07,ref08,ref09,ref18,ref19,ref20,ref21}; vision-based methods have mapped object attributes, geometry, or vision--language representations to stiffness or impedance settings \cite{ref10,ref11,ref13,ref14}. Related work on automatic switching, shared control, multi-stiffness interfaces, and teleoperated grasping has addressed mode selection, control-authority allocation, and gripper impedance based on tactile deformation or grip-safety information \cite{ref12,ref15,ref16,ref23,ref24,ref30}.

The present framework complements these directions by using a single object-conditioned strategy event to coordinate a strategy-level operating state across slave-side impedance, master-side haptic feedback, and gripper execution. The principal contribution is therefore a cross-subsystem, strategy-level configuration architecture together with a system-level evaluation of the resulting multi-channel coordination. At the software-architecture level, bounded queues, non-blocking polling, one-shot triggering, and strategy locking decouple low-rate semantic perception from the nominal 200-Hz supervisory execution path and prevent repeated parameter switching within a trial.

This design principle may be useful in semi-structured applications such as flexible sorting, remote maintenance, and dismantling, where a limited set of recurring object or task classes can be associated with predefined and experimentally screened operating strategies. Extending the framework to larger class sets would require systematic maintenance of the object-class-to-strategy mapping and may require a richer strategy space than the three levels evaluated here. Tactile or contact-state sensing could be added as a post-contact verification layer, enabling corrective updates after contact without requiring the visual process to operate within the nominal 200-Hz supervisory execution path.



\subsection{Scope and Future Extensions}
The formal-trial logs retained task duration, master-side trajectory, gripper input, active parameter state, and outcome labels, but not event-level object-class labels, confidence scores, complete visual-event histories, or synchronized strategy-event/contact timestamps. Consequently, online trigger correctness, fallback invocation, trigger-to-contact intervals, and completion of the strategy-level update before contact could not be unambiguously reconstructed retrospectively. The controlled 180-image test characterizes closed-set recognition under fixed imaging conditions; trial-level verification of contact-preparatory timing requires synchronized visual, strategy-event, and contact logging.

The experimental evidence was obtained from repeated measurements involving three operators, six known objects, translational master motion, a fixed end-effector orientation, prescribed object placement, and preassigned mode sequences without complete counterbalancing. The current implementation used a manually defined object-class-to-strategy mapping, so extension to a new object requires a detector label and an explicit strategy assignment. The findings therefore characterize the present platform and task within the tested participant sample. Broader evaluation should incorporate larger and more diverse operator cohorts, fully counterbalanced mode orders, and expanded motion, orientation, and placement conditions while preserving matched comparisons across experimental modes.

The reported parameter vectors were selected through qualitative engineering screening and should be interpreted as reproducible operating points for the present platform, objects, and task. Because the C--E contrast changed the master-side haptic and gripper-execution channels together, separate channel-level ablations are needed to resolve their individual and combined contributions. Outcome labels were based on direct observation, and the retained records did not include independent video-based re-rating or direct contact-quality and corrective-action measurements. Similarly, the retained haptic data characterize the commanded signal but not realized device output or saturation within the driver or device. Future experiments should combine synchronized visual, strategy-event, and contact logging with separate channel-level ablations, independent video-based outcome assessment, direct contact and realized haptic-output measurements, and systematic parameter optimization.
