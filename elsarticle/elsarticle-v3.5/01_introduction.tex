\section{Introduction}

Teleoperation leverages human judgment to perform remote manipulation tasks that remain difficult for autonomous robots, particularly in hazardous environments, flexible manufacturing, and unstructured settings. Haptic feedback conveys remote contact information to the operator and supports assessment of contact state, grasp stability, and slip. Previous studies have examined the stability and transparency of bilateral teleoperation and the adaptation of controllers to the environment, operator, and task \cite{ref01,ref02}. In practical systems, however, these control functions must operate in concert with visual perception, mechanical execution, and the human--machine interface.

For heterogeneous objects, a single parameter set must balance gentle contact with positioning and transfer stability. Lower stiffness and gripping force can reduce contact severity but may compromise positioning and transfer stability, whereas higher values may improve manipulation speed at the cost of greater impact or deformation risk. The six test objects---an apple, a banana, a paper cup, a bottle, a computer mouse, and a pair of scissors---were selected to represent diverse demands on compliance, haptic interaction, and gripper execution. This controlled object set enabled evaluation of coordinated parameter reconfiguration for heterogeneous grasping tasks.

Impedance control regulates the relationship among displacement, velocity, and interaction force, allowing a robot to remain compliant during contact \cite{ref03}. Fixed impedance is straightforward to implement but inevitably represents a compromise across objects. Variable-impedance methods instead adapt stiffness and damping according to task state, contact information, or learned policies, while accounting for the stability implications of time-varying parameters \cite{ref04,ref05}. In teleoperation, operators may adjust slave-side impedance directly \cite{ref06}, whereas tele-impedance approaches map human motion and impedance information to the remote robot \cite{ref07,ref08,ref09}. Such continuous adaptation commonly relies on human measurements, demonstration data, or post-contact feedback.

Visual information has also been used to select or adapt robot impedance. A vision-and-voice tele-impedance method selected slave-side impedance from object properties while retaining an operator confirmation or correction step \cite{ref10}, and visual-impedance control has combined visual and force information in a visual-feature space \cite{ref11}. At the workflow level, intention inference and task-oriented shared control have reduced manual decision-making \cite{ref12}. More recent approaches have derived stiffness from object geometry, material, and environmental relationships \cite{ref13} or generated task-relevant stiffness matrices from visual, gaze, and language inputs \cite{ref14}. These studies primarily translate task information into slave-side stiffness, impedance, or control-authority decisions.

Automatic switching and passive arbitration additionally determine how control authority is allocated and when it changes \cite{ref15,ref16}. Haptic interfaces have been evaluated for telemanipulation performance \cite{ref17}, while related work has addressed compliant assembly \cite{ref18}, human and environmental stiffness estimation \cite{ref19}, rotational impedance \cite{ref20}, scaling transitions \cite{ref21}, vision-based tactile feedback \cite{ref22}, multi-stiffness interfaces \cite{ref23}, delay-aware shared autonomy \cite{ref24}, and haptic support under restricted vision \cite{ref25}. These studies generally adapt individual control or interaction elements. By contrast, limited work has used object semantics as a common task condition to jointly configure the slave-side mechanical response, the operator-facing haptic interface, and gripper execution. The central question is therefore not whether vision can tune impedance, but whether a strategy-level semantic decision can configure these three mechatronic subsystems as a unified parameter bundle and provide task-level benefits beyond visual-semantic display, manual selection, and impedance-only scheduling.

To address this gap, we propose a vision-semantic, event-triggered multi-channel joint parameter-configuration framework for pre-contact parameter preparation in haptic teleoperation of heterogeneous objects. A three-level mapping from object semantics to an operational strategy and then to a joint parameter vector translates task information into coordinated settings for slave-side impedance, the master-side haptic interface, and gripper execution. After task initiation, the first detection above the confidence threshold with a valid strategy mapping triggers a one-time update to the target parameters. The selected strategy is then locked for the remainder of the trial to prevent repeated switching in response to subsequent detection fluctuations.

The central positioning of this work is a vision-semantic, event-triggered multi-channel joint parameter-configuration framework for pre-contact parameter preparation in haptic teleoperation of heterogeneous objects. The framework uses object semantics as a task prior and aims to make the object-conditioned parameter bundle available before interaction, rather than continuously adapting it after contact. Its online stage uses discrete, event-triggered updates rather than post-contact continuous optimization or persistent visual servoing. Asynchronous implementation and strategy locking keep low-rate perception outside the nominal supervisory control path; by avoiding complex online optimization, the framework enables interpretable and deployable system-level parameter configuration with a relatively low online computational burden.

The system-level evaluation uses five modes: fixed parameters, operator selection, full cross-channel reconfiguration, a visual-semantic cue with fixed parameters, and impedance-only scheduling. The C--D comparison assesses automatic reconfiguration beyond the displayed visual-semantic cue. The primary C--E comparison holds the visual-semantic mapping and slave-side impedance settings constant and tests the complete parameter bundle against impedance-only scheduling. Operator- and object-stratified results describe whether this system-level difference has a consistent direction. They do not identify the independent causal contributions of the haptic and gripper parameter groups.

The main contributions are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Event-triggered multi-channel joint parameter configuration for pre-contact preparation.} The framework extends object-dependent configuration from slave-side impedance alone to three jointly configured subsystems: the slave-side mechanical response, the operator-facing haptic interface, and gripper execution. A common operational strategy assigns a consistent parameter bundle; the joint configuration is not derived from a dynamic coupling model between channels.
\item \textbf{Three-level semantic mapping within a constrained parameter space.} Object classes are grouped into fragility-priority, balanced, and stability-priority operational strategies and mapped to a seven-dimensional joint parameter vector constrained by hardware capabilities, task risks, and the operating envelope established in preliminary testing.
\item \textbf{Asynchronous, event-triggered one-shot reconfiguration.} Image acquisition at 15 fps and independent YOLO11n inference communicate with a nominal 200-Hz supervisory update through bounded queues. Non-blocking polling prevents the supervisory thread from waiting for perception; strategy locking avoids repeated intra-trial switching caused by detection fluctuations; and an approximately 300-ms transition smooths changes in stiffness and the controller damping-scaling parameter.
\item \textbf{Human-in-the-loop system-level evaluation across five modes.} Five modes were implemented on an Omega.7--Panda platform to provide comparative evidence for visual-semantic display, manual strategy selection, impedance-only scheduling, and full cross-channel reconfiguration. The primary C--E comparison evaluates the complete parameter bundle at the system level and does not separate the individual effects of the added haptic-interface and gripper channels. The evaluation involved three operators, six objects, and 135 trials.
\end{enumerate}
